<td
style="text-align: left;"><code>transform-features (mCLR)</code><br />
<em>q2-gglasso</em></td>
<td style="text-align: left;">scale-invariant under per-sample
rescaling; suitable for proportions</td>
<td style="text-align: left;">The geometric mean is computed within each
sample over positive entries, so log(x/g) is invariant to a per-sample
rescaling. The global |min|+eps shift is a single additive constant
applied to the matrix and cancels in the covariance. On the top-300
table, mCLR from proportions, a global tenfold rescaling, and a random
per-sample rescaling agrees with the count-based result to maximum
absolute differences of 1.8e-15, 2.2e-15, and 1.8e-15, respectively. The
input must have an accepted feature-table type.</td>
<td style="text-align: left;"></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td
style="text-align: left;"><code>transform-features (CLR, --p-pseudo-count)</code><br />
<em>q2-gglasso</em></td>
<td style="text-align: left;">count-specific, not safe for
proportions</td>
<td style="text-align: left;">pseudo-count is declared Int, so the
smallest expressible floor is 1. On a proportions table every entry is
&lt;=1, so a floor of 1 exceeds every observed value: on the top-300
proportions table the maximum entry is 0.6250, so the floor exceeds it
by a factor of 1.6, and after renormalization the originally-zero cells
carry 99.65% of the mass. Edge Jaccard vs the count-based run at lambda1
= 0.80 drops to 0.557 (231 edges against 216).</td>
<td style="text-align: left;"></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td
style="text-align: left;"><code>transform-features (CLR, --p-coef)</code><br />
<em>q2-classo</em></td>
<td style="text-align: left;">requires care, safe if –p-coef is rescaled
with the data</td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><code>coef</code> is a Float, so a floor
below the smallest non-zero proportion can be expressed. CLR removes a
global scale: <span
class="math inline">\(\mathrm{CLR}(cX;cf)=\mathrm{CLR}(X;f)\)</span> for
a matching floor <span class="math inline">\(f\)</span> (verified
maximum absolute difference <span
class="math inline">\(1.8\times10^{-15}\)</span> for <span
class="math inline">\(c=10\)</span>). With an unmatched floor, the
transformed design differs by up to 7.12 in this check. Converting
counts to proportions rescales each sample separately, which no single
scalar floor can match exactly; mCLR is the scale-invariant route in
that case.</td>
<td style="text-align: left;"></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td style="text-align: left;"><code>calculate-covariance</code><br />
<em>q2-gglasso</em></td>
<td style="text-align: left;">scale-invariant given the transform</td>
<td style="text-align: left;">Operates on the already-transformed
matrix; method=’scaled’ divides by sqrt(diagonal) so any constant
additive shift or global multiplicative factor in the transform output
cancels. All scale dependence is inherited from the upstream transform,
not introduced here.</td>
<td style="text-align: left;"></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td
style="text-align: left;"><code>solve-problem (single / multiple / latent graphical lasso)</code><br />
<em>q2-gglasso</em></td>
<td style="text-align: left;">scale-invariant given the transform</td>
<td style="text-align: left;">Input is a p x p covariance/correlation;
the solver never sees counts. The penalty <span
class="math inline">\(\lambda_1\)</span> is on the scale of the input
covariance, so a transform that changes the covariance scale changes the
meaning of a given <span class="math inline">\(\lambda_1\)</span>.</td>
<td style="text-align: left;"></td>
</tr>

<td
style="text-align: left;"><code>regress (R1-R4 log-contrast)</code><br />
<em>q2-classo</em></td>
<td style="text-align: left;">scale-invariant on the transformed
design</td>
<td style="text-align: left;">The log-contrast constraint sum(beta)=0
makes the fit invariant to a per-sample multiplicative factor in the
original table (it becomes a per-sample additive constant in log space,
annihilated by the constraint). Verified on top-300 pH: mCLR on counts
and on proportions select the identical 15 features, whereas CLR with
coef fixed at 0.5 selects 15 on counts and 21 on proportions (Jaccard
0.385). Scale-safety therefore hinges entirely on the zero floor used
upstream.</td>
<td style="text-align: left;"></td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td
style="text-align: left;"><code>classify (log-contrast classification)</code><br />
<em>q2-classo</em></td>
<td style="text-align: left;">scale-invariant on the transformed
design</td>
<td style="text-align: left;">Same constraint argument as regress. In a
separate 54-sample top-300 scale check, a median-split elevation
contrast (27 vs 27) gives the same 13 mCLR-selected features from counts
and proportions. The CLR floor is not neutral for this loss, however:
<code>coef</code> in {0.01,0.1,0.5,1.0} on the count table selects 14,
16, 15 and 24 features respectively, and none of those sets equals the
mCLR set. Scale-safety holds; floor-independence does not.</td>
<td style="text-align: left;"><span
class="math inline">\(\circ\)</span></td>
</tr>

<td
style="text-align: left;"><code>transform-features then any downstream action on a table with fractional entries</code><br />
<em>q2-gglasso</em></td>
<td style="text-align: left;">blocked for CLR</td>
<td style="text-align: left;">The plugin’s own Int type on
